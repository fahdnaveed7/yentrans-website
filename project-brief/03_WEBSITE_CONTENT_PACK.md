## Article 1 — Reefer Logistics: What Exporters Should Confirm Before Booking

**Category:** Knowledge Hub · Reefer Logistics  
**Slug:** `reefer-logistics-exporter-checklist`  
**Excerpt:** A reefer booking is only one part of moving temperature-sensitive cargo. Here's what exporters should settle before the container arrives.  
**Status:** Draft

### A reefer is a machine before it is a container

Temperature-sensitive shipping starts with the product, not the vessel schedule. A frozen seafood shipment, chilled produce movement and temperature-controlled healthcare shipment can involve very different operating requirements. Before discussing a booking, the shipper and logistics team need to agree on what the cargo actually needs.

The first step is the product instruction: the required transport temperature, whether any ventilation or humidity settings apply, how the product was prepared, and who is responsible for checking condition at stuffing. The container's set point is not the same thing as rapidly cooling warm cargo. Product pre-cooling and stuffing practices belong in the planning discussion.

Equipment availability is the next decision. A reefer must be suitable for the cargo and the required journey. Pre-trip inspection information, power arrangements and equipment release timing should be discussed with the responsible parties. If the inland segment needs a genset, that requirement has to be planned before collection.

Routing also matters. Transshipment, plug-in arrangements, terminal handling windows and port cutoffs can affect operational feasibility. The lowest available rate may not be the best movement if a longer route introduces unacceptable cargo or schedule considerations.

Documentation should reflect the agreed instructions. Shipper requirements, booking details and shipping documents need to be consistent, and responsibilities for temperature records and escalation should be clear.

**A practical pre-booking discussion should cover:** commodity and condition; frozen or chilled requirements; shipper instructions; equipment type; stuffing location; pre-cooling responsibilities; inland power; routing; and destination handover.

The goal is not to eliminate every variable. It is to identify the important ones before a small omission becomes a costly disruption. For cargo-specific planning, discuss requirements with the Yentrans team and confirm the final handling obligations in writing.

## Article 2 — FCL or LCL? Choosing the Right Container Movement

**Category:** Knowledge Hub · Ocean Freight  
**Slug:** `fcl-vs-lcl-shipping-guide`  
**Excerpt:** Container size is only one consideration. Compare control, handling, cost components and transit requirements before choosing FCL or LCL.  
**Status:** Draft

### The right answer is not always the cheapest line item

FCL, or Full Container Load, is a movement in which the shipment uses a container allocated to a particular booking. LCL, or Less than Container Load, involves cargo consolidated with other consignments. Both can be effective, but they solve different planning problems.

A shipper may start by comparing freight prices. That makes sense, but the cost comparison should include more than the ocean segment. LCL movements can involve origin and destination consolidation charges, cargo handling, documentation and delivery arrangements. FCL movements can bring container-related costs, inland equipment needs and port or detention considerations. The exact charges vary by trade lane and provider.

Handling and timing also deserve attention. A consolidated shipment may depend on cutoff dates, warehouse receiving, loading and deconsolidation processes. A full-container shipment has its own container pickup and return requirements but can reduce some intermediate handling steps.

Product characteristics can change the decision. Fragile items, cargo requiring careful isolation, non-standard packaging or high-volume repeat shipments may benefit from a dedicated container. Smaller, less time-sensitive consignments may be better suited to consolidation.

It is also worth discussing the trade-off between a full container that is not completely utilized and an LCL movement that carries extra handling and destination charges. The answer depends on the lane, dimensions, weight, volume and delivery requirement.

When asking for a quote, share the cargo's packed dimensions, gross weight, commodity, origin, destination and ready date. A useful comparison should state what is included, what is excluded and what happens at the destination.

**The best choice is the one that fits the cargo and the total movement, not merely the smallest number beside “ocean freight.”**

## Article 3 — House Bill vs. Master Bill of Lading

**Category:** Knowledge Hub · Documentation  
**Slug:** `house-bill-vs-master-bill-of-lading`  
**Excerpt:** Understand which party issues each document and why the distinction matters in the way a shipment is structured.  
**Status:** Draft

### Two documents can describe one shipment at different levels

The bill of lading is central to ocean shipping, but the terms House Bill of Lading (HBL) and Master Bill of Lading (MBL) are often used as though they were interchangeable. They are not.

A Master Bill of Lading is generally issued by the ocean carrier to the party booking carriage with that carrier, often the NVOCC or freight forwarder in a consolidated arrangement. A House Bill of Lading is generally issued by an NVOCC or forwarding entity, where authorized, to its customer under the relevant contractual structure.

The party issuing a bill matters because it helps identify the parties to the contract of carriage and the terms that apply. A customer may see an HBL while a carrier issues an MBL for the same physical container movement. Names, references and shipment details need to be reconciled carefully across the documentation chain.

Why might this matter to an exporter or importer? Banks may have specific documentary instructions in letters of credit. Consignees and customs authorities may require particular information. Destination release procedures and contractual responsibilities also depend on how the shipment is structured.

A practical discussion before booking should cover whether a house bill will be used, who issues each document, the named shipper and consignee, any documentary credit requirements, the notify party, and the destination release procedure.

These choices should not be improvised after the vessel sails. Once documents enter a banking or customs process, even a minor inconsistency may create delays or additional costs.

Yentrans supports customers by coordinating shipping documentation and the necessary carrier instructions according to the selected shipment structure. **The ocean carrier normally issues the MBL; a properly authorized NVOCC may issue the HBL.**

### The port is a connection point, not the final answer

For exporters and importers moving cargo between India and the Middle East, ocean freight is a visible part of the total journey. Yet the decisive operational questions often arise before a container reaches port or after it is discharged.

At origin, cargo readiness must be aligned with container availability and vessel cutoffs. Booking details need to agree with shipping instructions, and inland transport needs sufficient time to reach the terminal. Special cargo requirements, such as reefer power or non-standard equipment, should be recognized before the vehicle is dispatched.

At destination, customs documentation, consignee instructions, terminal processes and delivery availability can be just as important. A fast ocean transit does not automatically mean a fast final delivery if the receiving documents or inland arrangements are incomplete.

Shipment planning benefits from a coordinated timeline: cargo ready date; equipment release; pickup and stuffing; documentation cutoff; vessel departure and arrival; destination clearance; and final delivery. Each of these stages has a responsible party and a point at which changes become expensive.

## Article 5 — Project Cargo: Questions to Answer Before Requesting a Rate

**Category:** Knowledge Hub · Project Logistics  
**Slug:** `project-cargo-before-rate`  
**Excerpt:** Oversized cargo planning starts with dimensions, drawings, lifting and route feasibility — not a published standard container price.  
**Status:** Draft

### A project shipment begins with technical information

An ordinary carton can often be described by quantity, dimensions and gross weight. An oversized machine, industrial component or large piece of equipment needs a more detailed conversation.

The first requirement is accurate technical data. This may include a dimensional drawing, packed gross weight, lifting points, center of gravity, packaging method and photographs. If the cargo consists of multiple pieces, each item needs its own specification.

Then comes the movement plan. Can the cargo travel on a standard road trailer? Are there bridge, turning-radius, height or axle-load restrictions? Does the port have suitable lifting equipment? Does the shipment require a flat rack, open-top container, breakbulk solution or other specialized arrangement?

Lifting and securing cannot be an afterthought. The parties responsible for packing, lifting, survey and cargo securing should be identified early, alongside any requirements for permits or approvals. Insurance and contractual responsibilities should reflect the actual movement.

A transport offer prepared without reliable technical information is often only a preliminary estimate. Changes in weight, dimensions or equipment can change the entire route and price.

For shippers, the best first step is to gather the drawings and practical site details before asking for a freight rate. That makes it easier for logistics teams and specialist partners to assess feasible options and their limitations.

**A realistic project cargo plan is built from the physical facts of the shipment.**
